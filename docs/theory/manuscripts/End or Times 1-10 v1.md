### Chapter 1: The Day the Sky Caught Fire  
(4,800 words)

#### 1.1 The Magnet That Failed
Between 12,900 and 12,800 years ago the Earth’s magnetic field collapsed to between 5 and 20 % of its normal strength—the Gothenburg excursion superimposed on the dying embers of the Laschamp event forty-one millennia earlier. For perhaps four human generations the planet was naked to the cosmos.

We know because every ice core from Greenland to Antarctica records the same thing: beryllium-10 and carbon-14 rain down in quantities not seen again until the hydrogen-bomb tests of the 1960s. Cosmic rays that are normally deflected arrived in a flood. The upper atmosphere became a particle accelerator.

When the shield drops, three things happen fast:
- The aurora descends to the equator and turns daytime green.
- The Van Allen belts dump their trapped particles into the atmosphere.
- Every solar flare becomes a planet-killer.

#### 1.2 The Airburst Swarm
Fifty-four archaeological sites on six continents share an identical calling card: a carbon-rich “black mat” 2–30 cm thick, loaded with nanodiamonds, magnetic microspherules, and platinum-group anomalies a thousand times above background. The layer is razor-sharp in the stratigraphy—nothing lived on top of it for centuries.

Abu Hureyra, Syria: a village of 300 people vaporized so fast that the wheat in their grinding bowls fused into glass. Human skulls exploded from internal steam; the fragments were coated in impact glass that rained back as green droplets.

Gainey, Michigan: Clovis hunters buried under two metres of charred bone and melted magnetite.

Lake Hind, Manitoba: an airburst large enough to excavate a 10-km crater in the bedrock—yet no crater, because the projectile detonated 8 km above the surface.

The total energy release is conservatively estimated at 10–20 million megatons. Ten thousand times the world’s current nuclear arsenal, delivered in hours.

#### 1.3 The Fire That Ate Ten Percent of Everything Alive
Charcoal influx in lake beds and ocean cores jumps by factors of 20–100. Ten percent of the planet’s terrestrial biomass flashed to carbon between 12.87 and 12.83 ka. In the Americas the signal is so strong it is called the “Younger Dryas boundary carbon apocalypse layer.”

Imagine every forest, every prairie, every peat bog igniting within the same lunar month. The smoke column reached the stratosphere and stayed there for years. Sunlight was reduced by 30–50 %. Global temperature fell 5–12 °C in a decade.

#### 1.4 The Water That Came All at Once
Meltwater Pulse 1B added between 14 and 20 metres of water to the world ocean in less than 500 years, with the steepest rise—perhaps four metres—occurring in a single lifetime. Coral reefs off Tahiti and Barbados record a dead-vertical jump in sea level that no amount of simple ice-sheet collapse can explain.

The mechanism: subglacial lakes the size of modern Lake Victoria, superheated by impact energy, detonated outward under the Laurentide and Fennoscandian ice sheets. The flood ran under the ice at 90 km/h, lifting entire ice lobes and dumping them into the North Atlantic as ice-armadas the size of nations.

Tsunami run-up scars on the sides of Scottish mountains reach 25 metres above modern sea level. The Bahama Banks show chevron dunes of coral rubble pointing inland—deposited by waves that crossed 100 km of open ocean and still carried house-sized boulders.

Twenty-five million square kilometres of coastal plain—home to the densest human populations of the Ice Age—were submerged forever.

#### 1.5 The Ledger of the Dead
North America lost 35 of 44 large mammal genera in less than two centuries. South America lost 50 of 60. Australia had already been wounded by humans; the final coup de grâce came from the sky. In Africa and southern Asia the die-off was “only” 20–30 %, because they were farther from ground zero.

The Clovis culture, the Solutrean-derived maritime culture of the Atlantic façade, the pre-Jōmon seafarers of Japan—gone. Entire technological horizons erased overnight.

### Chapter 2: The Long Night of Forgetting  
(4,200 words)

#### 2.1 The Empty Coasts
Walk any beach from New Jersey to Perth today and you are standing on ground that was dry land for most of human existence. Below –80 m on the continental shelf lie drowned river valleys, fish weirs, and harpoon points. Between –120 m and –60 m there is almost nothing. A 60-metre vertical band of human history simply missing.

That band is the Younger Dryas coast. The people who lived there never made it to higher ground with their tools. The few artefacts that surface in fishing nets are water-rolled and unstratified—corpses of a world.

#### 2.2 The Genetic Scars
Native American genomes still carry the signature: a bottleneck so severe that some tribes have less genetic diversity than a single modern European village. The same signal appears in Aboriginal Australians whose ancestors walked Sahul when it was twice its present size. In the Andaman Islands the Onge and Jarawa are living fossils of the coastal exodus that never completed.

#### 2.3 The Century That Took Four Hundred Years
It was not dark because the sun failed. It was dark because there was nothing left to burn and no one left to tend the fire.

Pollen cores show grass and sagebrush for centuries where there had been forest. Lake levels in the American West dropped 100 metres, then rose 150 metres, then dropped again as the climate flip-flopped between stadial and interstadial like a dying animal breathing its last.

The survivors ate bone marrow cracked from frozen carcasses. They ate their dogs. They ate each other when the dogs ran out. Cannibalism sites from Colorado to Belgium date precisely to the first three centuries of the Younger Dryas.

#### 2.4 The First Farmers Were Refugees
Agriculture did not begin because someone got clever with seeds. It began because the refugees on the Anatolian plateau and in the hills of the Zagros had nothing else. The wild wheat was there, the wild goats were there, and the coast that had fed their grandparents for a thousand generations was now 400 km away and 120 m deep.

Domestication is what happens when you are too afraid to move.
